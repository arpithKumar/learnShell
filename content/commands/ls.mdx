`ls` is usually the very first command anyone runs in a new directory,
and for good reason: it lists what's there. On its own it just prints
filenames, which is fine for a quick glance, but the real value shows
up once you start combining flags.

`-a` reveals "hidden" files — anything whose name starts with a dot,
like `.gitignore` or `.env`, which `ls` otherwise omits to keep
everyday listings clean. `-l` switches to a detailed, one-file-per-line
format showing permissions, owner, size, and modification date. Add
`-h` alongside `-l` to make those sizes human-readable (so you see "1.2K"
instead of "1234"), since raw byte counts are slow to parse mentally.

Put together, `ls -lah` is close to a universal habit among shell
users: everything, in detail, with readable sizes. `ls -lt` is the
other combination worth knowing early — it sorts by modification time
instead of name, which makes "what did I just touch" instantly visible
at the top of the list.
