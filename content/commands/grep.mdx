`grep` (Global Regular Expression Print) scans its input line by line
and prints whichever lines match a given pattern. It's one of the most
heavily used Unix utilities in existence, and for many people it's the
very first regular-expression tool they ever learn, since the basic
case — `grep "word" file.txt` — needs no regex knowledge at all to be
immediately useful.

Three flags turn plain `grep` into something you'll reach for
constantly: `-i` for case-insensitive matching, `-n` to show line
numbers alongside each result, and `-r` to search an entire folder
recursively instead of one file. `grep -rn "TODO" ./src` is a genuinely
common way to survey a codebase for outstanding work.

Beyond literal text, `grep` understands regular expressions, which let
you match patterns rather than exact strings — digits, optional
characters, alternatives. The `-E` flag enables "extended" regex syntax,
which is closer to what you'd see in most programming languages and
avoids having to escape characters like `+` and `?` with backslashes.
`grep` is also frequently the first half of a longer pipeline — finding
candidate lines that get fed into `sed`, `awk`, or `cut` for further
processing.
