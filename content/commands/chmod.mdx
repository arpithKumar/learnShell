Every file on a Unix-like system carries a set of permissions: who can
read it, who can write to it, and who can execute it as a program.
`chmod` ("change mode") is how you adjust those permissions, and you'll
most often meet it for one specific reason — making a script you just
wrote or downloaded actually runnable.

Permissions can be set two ways. The symbolic form reads almost like
English: `chmod +x file` adds execute permission for everyone,
`chmod u+w file` adds write permission for just the owner. The numeric
form represents the same thing as a sum: read = 4, write = 2,
execute = 1, added together separately for owner, group, and others.
`755` breaks down as `7` (4+2+1, full access) for the owner, and `5`
(4+1, read and execute but not write) for everyone else.

If you're new to this, it's worth resisting the urge to just throw
`777` at a permissions error to make it go away — that grants
read/write/execute to literally everyone who can reach the file, which
is rarely actually what the situation calls for. Take the extra moment
to figure out which specific permission was actually missing.
