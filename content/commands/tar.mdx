`tar` ("tape archive," a name that's outlived actual tapes by decades) bundles a set of files and directories into a single file, preserving their structure and permissions. It's the standard way Unix-like systems package and distribute multiple files as one — most software you download as a `.tar.gz` or `.tgz` was packaged with `tar`.

On its own, `tar` only combines files; it doesn't compress them. Combined with `-z` (gzip) or `-j` (bzip2), it both bundles and compresses in one step, which is why archives are almost always created and extracted with one of those flags included. The three letters that matter most are `c` (create), `x` (extract), and `f` (the archive filename follows) — `tar -czf` to create a compressed archive, `tar -xzf` to extract one.

Because extraction can silently overwrite files in the current directory, it's worth building the habit of checking an archive's contents with `tar -tf archive.tar.gz` before extracting anything you didn't create yourself.
